title: Viewers
description: How viewers turn audio into visualizations, and how renderers and shaders change their appearance.

An [audio source](/sonoscope/understanding/audio-sources/) provides samples. A **viewer** uses those samples to show something about the recording. It also uses the [viewport](/sonoscope/understanding/viewports/) to know which time or frequency range to show. You can attach several viewers to one [`Sonoscope`](/sonoscope/understanding/sonoscope/) so they share a source and stay aligned when you pan or zoom.

## What can you view?

Sonoscope has two built-in viewers:

| Viewer | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| [`WaveformViewer`](/sonoscope/reference/core/classes/waveformviewer/) | How the signal changes over time. |
| [`SpectrogramViewer`](/sonoscope/reference/core/classes/spectrogramviewer/) | How the frequencies in the signal change over time. |

A waveform and spectrogram can show the same source in different ways. The playhead is a separate overlay that marks playback time.

Viewers have settings for the data or view they produce. For example, a spectrogram's window size changes how much audio is analyzed at a time, while its frequency scale changes where frequencies appear on the vertical axis. Try the [waveform](/sonoscope/demos/waveform/) and [spectrogram](/sonoscope/demos/spectrogram/) demos to see them together.

## Locators show where you are

**Locators** show where you are in the recording. A time ruler marks the visible time range; a frequency ruler marks the visible frequencies. They share the viewport with viewers, so their marks move when you pan or zoom. You can also attach navigation controls to a ruler to let people drag or zoom there.

| Locator | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| [`TimeRulerLocator`](/sonoscope/reference/core/classes/timerulerlocator/) | Time marks for the visible range. |
| [`FrequencyRulerLocator`](/sonoscope/reference/core/classes/frequencyrulerlocator/) | Frequency marks alongside a spectrogram. |

The [rulers demo](/sonoscope/demos/rulers/) shows them alongside viewers.

## Renderers draw the view

A **renderer** decides how a viewer or locator draws. Changing a renderer does not change the recording or the visible range.

- A waveform can use Canvas2D or WebGL2 drawing, or show its peaks as bars with the `"bars"` renderer.
- A spectrogram can draw its computed data with Canvas2D or WebGL2. Its `"auto"` setting tries WebGL2 and falls back to Canvas2D when needed.
- Time and frequency rulers can draw ordinary `"ticks"` or segmented `"boxes"`.

For example, these two waveform viewers show the same source using different drawing styles:

```ts
scope.createWaveform(lineCanvas, { renderer: "canvas2d" });
scope.createWaveform(barCanvas, { renderer: "bars" });
```

You can also register your own renderer. The [Plugins overview](/sonoscope/plugins/) shows other ways to draw spectrograms, including ASCII text and WebGL effects.

## Where shaders fit

WebGL2 renderers use small programs called **shaders** to draw with the GPU. In the spectrogram viewer, the audio samples are first turned into spectrogram data. The WebGL2 renderer then gives that data to a shader program to decide how it appears on screen. The shader changes the picture, not the underlying audio or its frequency analysis.

The built-in WebGL2 spectrogram program is `"normal"`. A custom WebGL2 program can change the look, for example by drawing [halftone dots](/sonoscope/plugins/halftone/) or [topographic contours](/sonoscope/plugins/topographic/). Programs are a way to customize the WebGL2 spectrogram renderer; they are not a separate kind of audio source or compute backend. The [`registerSpectrogramProgram()` reference](/sonoscope/reference/core/functions/registerspectrogramprogram/) describes the extension point.

If you want to know why a spectrogram viewer works in pieces rather than calculating the whole recording at once, continue to [Why spectrograms use tiles](/sonoscope/understanding/why-tiling/).
