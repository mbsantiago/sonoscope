Once you have an [audio source](/sonoscope/understanding/audio-sources/), you can visualize its samples to get a better sense of what's in the recording.
A **viewer** uses those samples to show something about the audio. It also uses the [viewport](/sonoscope/understanding/viewports/) to know which time or frequency range to show.
You can use several viewers with the same source to see different aspects of the audio at once. [`Sonoscope`](/sonoscope/understanding/sonoscope/) makes it easy to connect them and keep them aligned when you pan or zoom.

## What can you view?

Sonoscope has two built-in viewers:

| Viewer | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| [`WaveformViewer`](/sonoscope/reference/core/classes/waveformviewer/) | How the strength of the audio signal changes over time. |
| [`SpectrogramViewer`](/sonoscope/reference/core/classes/spectrogramviewer/) | How the frequencies in the signal change over time. |

A waveform and a spectrogram show the same audio in different ways.

Viewers have settings for the data or view they produce.
For example, a spectrogram's window size changes how much audio is analyzed at a time, while its frequency scale changes where frequencies appear on the vertical axis.
Try the [waveform](/sonoscope/demos/waveform/) and [spectrogram](/sonoscope/demos/spectrogram/) demos to see them together.

## Locators show where you are

To see which part of the recording you're looking at, you can use a **locator**.
A time ruler marks the visible time range, while a frequency ruler marks the frequencies shown.
Locators share the viewport with viewers, so their marks move when you pan or zoom.
You can also attach navigation controls to a ruler to let people drag or zoom there.

| Locator | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| [`TimeRulerLocator`](/sonoscope/reference/core/classes/timerulerlocator/) | Time marks for the visible range. |
| [`FrequencyRulerLocator`](/sonoscope/reference/core/classes/frequencyrulerlocator/) | Frequency marks alongside a spectrogram. |

The [rulers demo](/sonoscope/demos/rulers/) shows them alongside viewers.

## Why use different renderers?

Often there is more than one way to visualize the same thing. A waveform, for example, can show changes in amplitude as a continuous envelope or as a series of bars.

<WaveformRenderersFigure />

In Sonoscope, a **renderer** is the part that draws a viewer or locator. You can change it without changing the audio or the visible range. Every built-in viewer and locator has a default renderer, so you usually don't need to choose one. You might want a different renderer when:

- You want another way to show the same information. For example, you can draw a waveform with `"envelope"` for a line and envelope, or `"bars"` for separate peaks. Time and frequency rulers can use ordinary `"ticks"` or segmented `"boxes"`.
- You need a different drawing technology. Browsers offer more than one way to draw on a canvas: Canvas2D provides 2D drawing tools, while WebGL2 uses the graphics processor. WebGL2 can be faster for a detailed spectrogram, but it isn't available everywhere. The spectrogram's `"auto"` setting tries WebGL2 first and uses Canvas2D when WebGL2 isn't available.

For example, these two waveform viewers show the same source in different styles:

```ts
// Draw the waveform as a line and peak envelope.
scope.createWaveform(lineCanvas, { renderer: "envelope" });

// Draw its peaks as bars.
scope.createWaveform(barCanvas, { renderer: "bars" });
```

If the built-in renderers don't give you the look or drawing method you need, you can register your own. The [Plugins overview](/sonoscope/plugins/) shows other ways to draw spectrograms, including ASCII text and WebGL effects.

## Spectrogram WebGL renderer and shaders

When the spectrogram uses WebGL2, its renderer passes the spectrogram data calculated from the audio samples to a small program called a **shader**, which decides how to draw it on screen.

The built-in `"normal"` program draws a spectrogram like the one in the [spectrogram demo](/sonoscope/demos/spectrogram/). Examples of custom shader programs are [halftone dots](/sonoscope/plugins/halftone/) and [topographic contours](/sonoscope/plugins/topographic/).

You can also register your own WebGL2 program for the spectrogram viewer. See the [`registerSpectrogramProgram()` reference](/sonoscope/reference/core/functions/registerspectrogramprogram/) for details.

If you want to know why a spectrogram viewer works in pieces rather than calculating the whole recording at once, continue to [Why spectrograms use tiles](/sonoscope/understanding/why-tiling/).
